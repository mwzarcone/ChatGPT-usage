How the modeled numbers are made
  1. Start with a national estimate of US monthly ChatGPT users from third-party sources. OpenAI does not publish one.
  2. Split it across states by population.
  3. Tilt each state up or down by its latest quarterly rank (July to September 2026) in OpenAI Signals' per-capita message ranking, then rescale so the states add back to the national total.

So the state figures show how users are likely distributed, not a count. Scenarios add assumed national growth, extra catch-up room for lower-ranked states, and a small tilt up or down for states whose rank has been climbing or slipping. See Method and sources for every assumption and reference.

What does this map show?

Each state is shaded by modeled monthly ChatGPT users, either as a share of residents or as total users. Share of residents shows how deeply ChatGPT has worked its way into everyday life in a place. Total users shows the sheer size of the audience. They tell different stories: a small state can lead on share and still be a modest audience, while a large state can have a lower share and still hold millions of people.

For anyone thinking about where people are likely to see and act on ads inside AI conversations, share of residents is the closer clue to habit and familiarity, and total users is the closer clue to reach. Neither measures ad inventory, ad delivery or results, and both are modeled from OpenAI's latest quarterly ranking (July to September 2026) rather than counted. Because the ranking is now published quarterly, the dashboard can also show which states have been climbing or slipping since early 2025. A rank is relative, so it can move because other states changed.

The Rank trend view swaps the shading for direction: green for states climbing the ranking since early 2025, red for states slipping, gray for steady ones, with deeper color for bigger moves. It shows where habits appear to be strengthening or fading relative to other states, not how many people use ChatGPT there.

How should I read this table?

The table puts the map's numbers side by side so states can be sorted and compared. The rank on the left depends on how you sort. By % of residents it is OpenAI's ranking of messages per resident for July to September 2026, the most direct measure of how often people in a state actually turn to ChatGPT. By modeled users it ranks the size of each audience. The user counts and shares are built from the OpenAI ranking, so they move together. What differs is whether you care about a state's size or its intensity.

Sort by share of residents to find places where ChatGPT is already part of daily routines, and by modeled users to find where the largest crowds are. Where those two lists disagree is where the trade-off between depth and scale sits.

OpenAI's ranking is a quarterly figure that uses 2023 Census population, and state results come from a small part of the sample, so they are noisier than the national figures. A gap of a place or two between neighboring states should not be read as a real difference.

The rank trend column shows whether a state's per-capita rank has been climbing or slipping across the seven quarters from early 2025 to July to September 2026. It comes from OpenAI's own quarterly state rankings, so it is measured rather than taken from third-party lists. A state is called climbing or slipping when a fitted trend moves it 5 or more places. Ranks are relative: a state can climb because others slowed, and single quarters are noisy, so treat it as a direction, not a size. The full references are on the Method and sources tab.

What does this chart show?

This follows the US against other countries on how much each resident uses ChatGPT, quarter by quarter. It is a ranking, so a falling line does not mean fewer Americans are using it. It means people elsewhere picked it up faster. Some markets that started lower, such as Japan and South Korea, have climbed quickly, and the US has swung between roughly 40th and 58th, ending below where it started in early 2025.

It is the sober counterweight to the state map: the US is a large, established audience, but it is no longer where per-person usage is rising fastest. Audience behavior is shifting more quickly in other markets, which limits how much new reach a US-only view can promise.

Work-related share of messages

US vs all countries, monthly, consumer accounts.

What does this chart show?

Each month, OpenAI's automated classifiers label a sample of consumer ChatGPT messages as work-related or not. The chart shows the work-related share of those messages for the US and for all countries combined. The other messages are personal use, such as learning, advice, writing for yourself or entertainment. People do not read the messages.

Over the period shown, the work-related share declined, and the US tracks the world closely.

A falling share of work messages means personal use grew faster, not that work use shrank.

The shift toward personal use suggests that ChatGPT's growing audience is increasingly using it in everyday, non-work contexts. That's relevant to which kinds of ad contexts fit. It doesn't say anything about specific states or about ad performance.

Limits: The data covers consumer accounts of adults only, so business use is understated. It is a share of messages, not of users, so heavy users count for more. The US line comes from a sampled subset with privacy noise added, so small month-to-month moves are partly noise.

What does this show?

OpenAI now publishes each state's per-capita ranking every quarter, so the dashboard can show movement and not only a snapshot. A rising line means people in that state are turning to ChatGPT more often relative to other states. A falling line means the opposite, which can happen because other states sped up.

The pattern points to adoption spreading beyond the usual tech-heavy places: some smaller and less urban states have been climbing while several western and northeastern states slipped. Newer adopters often use ChatGPT differently from early ones, so where habits are forming now may not match where usage started.

What does this chart show?

Each line follows one chatbot's share of all visits to chatbot websites worldwide, from October 2025 to September 2026. Similarweb measures the traffic and Visual Capitalist charted it. A point of share is one percent of all chatbot visits.

ChatGPT is still well ahead, but its share fell from 71.8% to 56.4%. Gemini rose from 13.6% to 25.0% and Claude from 2.3% to 9.6%. DeepSeek, Grok, Copilot and Perplexity all slipped.

In everyday terms, people are spreading their chatbot habits across more than one assistant, while the largest single audience still goes to ChatGPT. That matters for anyone thinking about where attention gathers: ChatGPT still offers the broadest reach, and a growing share of AI sessions now happens elsewhere. ChatGPT began showing ads to free users in early 2026, and Claude has positioned itself as ad-free. The data shows what happened to traffic over that period, not why.

Limits: This shows shares, not totals, so a smaller share can still mean more visits if the whole market grew. It counts visits to chatbot websites worldwide, so it leaves out app use and it is not specific to the US. Visits are not people. The visit estimates are scaled from one published total, so treat them as approximate. It says nothing about ad delivery or ad performance.

Three leaders, three readings

ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude at October 2025, April 2026 and September 2026. The other chatbots were not reported for April.

What changed along the way?

The shift was not a steady slide. ChatGPT fell to 53.7% by April 2026, then recovered to 56.4% by September. Gemini climbed to 26.7% in April and eased back to 25.0%. Claude kept rising, from 2.3% to 8.0% to 9.6%, but more slowly after April.

The rebound is only five months old, which is too short to call a new trend. What it does show is that audience habits are still moving, and the order of the top three has been stable while the gaps between them narrowed.

Limits: Three readings cannot show what happened between them, and the April figures cover only these three chatbots.

How should I read these scenarios?

The low, base and high cases show the same starting audience growing at three different speeds over the coming months. They are what-if exercises, not predictions, and the growth rates are assumptions chosen to bracket a plateau, modest growth and a stronger pickup.

The useful comparison is the spread between the cases. If the plateau case is closer to reality, reach grows mostly through new habits among existing users. If the high case is, the audience itself is expanding. Either way, more users does not automatically mean more people paying attention to ads, so treat these as audience-size scenarios, not ad-delivery scenarios.

Competition matters too. The share of chatbot web visits going to ChatGPT fell over the past year (see the Chatbot share tab), which makes the plateau and base cases easier to defend than the high case.

Where the model adds the most users, or grows fastest.

What does this map show?

This shades each state by how many users it is modeled to add, or how fast it grows, under the scenario and horizon chosen above. Users added favors large states, because a small percentage on a big base is still a lot of people. Growth percent favors smaller or lower-usage states, because they have more room to catch up.

The two views mirror two questions about consumer behavior: where the biggest new crowds could form, and where habits are changing fastest. The catch-up effect is an assumption built into the model, not something measured, so read fast growth in a low-usage state as room to grow rather than proven momentum.

What does this chart show?

The ranking lists the states the model expects to add the most users. It is dominated by the biggest states because size does most of the work, so the order changes little between cases. What changes is how large the bars are.

How do I use this table?

This is the full list behind the map and chart for the selected scenario. It is useful for checking a specific state, or for sorting by growth percent to spot places the model treats as fast-moving. Because every state's growth traces back to the same national assumption plus two small tilts, catch-up and rank trend, differences between neighboring states are small and should not be over-read.

It works best for comparing states with each other rather than quoting any single number. A state's figure here is a modeled estimate with a wide margin of error, built from OpenAI's latest quarterly ranking.

How the estimates are built

Start with a national estimate of US monthly active users from third-party sources (OpenAI does not publish one). Allocate it to states by population, then tilt each state by its latest (July to September 2026) per-capita message rank from OpenAI Signals. The result is a modeled estimate, not a count.

Scenarios apply a national growth case, give lower-ranked states more catch-up room, and add a small tilt up or down for states whose per-capita rank has been climbing or slipping. State growth is rescaled so the total matches the national case.

What this cannot tell you

OpenAI's state data is a rank, not a user count, so state growth is only inferred from how ranks moved across seven quarters, and ranks are relative: a state can climb because others slowed. The mid-2025 fast-adopting lists used in the first version are no longer used. National user counts are third-party estimates. Data covers consumer accounts of adults and understates business use. Scenario growth rates are analyst assumptions.

Why these assumptions?

The growth rates are meant to bracket what the evidence supports: recent signs that US usage has leveled off, a modest pace overall, and an upside case for renewed momentum. The catch-up tilt reflects a pattern seen globally, where lower-usage markets tend to gain faster as habits spread. The momentum tilt now follows each state's measured rank trend.

Changing any of them moves every projection, which makes them the first place to look when a result seems surprising. They reflect judgment, which is why they are listed openly rather than buried.
